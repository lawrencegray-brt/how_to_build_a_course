# Contact-Hour Companion — Sizing a Module to Its Time

*The tool for two moments in the build: the **coarse fit-check** in §3.5 (Decompose Into Modules) — a ballpark size check from a module's objectives and depth, before any materials exist — and the **precise time budget** in §5 (Structure the Module), once the materials are real and you can sum actual minutes. Either way it estimates how long a module's work takes, so you can check it fits your container. Built on the Georgetown SCS Contact Hour Assessment, **re-pointed for short professional workshops** (not 15-week academic courses).*

---

## Read this first: these are not academic courses

The original Georgetown tool sizes a **15-week graded academic course** by its *weekly out-of-class workload* — a 7–9 hr/week target full of discussion-board posts, reflection papers, and HBR readings. **Your courses are different:**

- They're **defined by contact time**, not weekly homework load. The session length *is* the container.
- They're **short and varied** — a 1.5-hour intro, a 3-hour workshop, a 5-week 2-hr/week series.
- They're **hands-on and ungraded** — practice and demos, not graded discussion posts.

So you use the tool's **mechanism** (estimate time, sum, compare to the container) and its **reading-speed benchmarks**, but you **drop the academic furniture** (discussion-post counts, reflection-paper hours, weekly-workload targets). The question is simply: **does what I planned fit the time I actually have?**

## The tool works in both directions

A "container" is your course's real schedule, measured in **total contact hours.** You don't have to use any standard format — **design whatever shape fits your goal.** This tool just lets you reason about time both ways:

- **Time-first** — *"I have this much time. What can I realistically cover?"* (Fit the content to the container.)
- **Content-first** — *"I want to teach this. How much time will it take?"* (Let the content size the container.)

Most builds bounce between the two until content and time agree.

### Example formats (these are formats I have used — yours can be anything)

| Format | In-class contact | + Out-of-class | Total | Rough # modules |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Intro to a topic | 1.5 hr | — | 1.5 hr | 1 |
| Workshop | 3 hr | — | 3 hr | 1 |
| Lecture series | 5 × 1 hr | — | 5 hr | ~5 |
| 5-week series | 5 × 2 hr | optional | ~10 hr | ~5 |
| 8-week course | 8 × 3 hr | 8 × 1 hr | ~32 hr | ~8 |
| 12-week course | 12 × 4 hr | (in the 4) | ~48 hr | ~12 |

These are **starting points, not a menu.** The module count falls out of (total hours ÷ how long each coherent chunk needs) — a 3-hour workshop is likely one module; a 12-week course, about twelve.

## The formula

> **Hours = Total quantity ÷ Average-per-hour**

For each reading, video, demo, or practice activity: estimate how much a student gets through in an hour (**average per hour**), how much there is (**total**), and divide.

## Benchmark rates (starting estimates — tune to your audience)

**Readings** *(from the Georgetown tool — pages/hour, slower for denser material):*

| Material | Pages/hour | What the student is doing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dense / technical (textbook-style) | ~20 | **Studying** — reading to learn and retain |
| Article (HBR-style) | ~30 | Reading closely, but not to memorize |
| Web / lighter reading | ~35 | **Reading** — following the argument once |

*Name the purpose or the number means nothing: it's the single biggest factor here, far bigger than how fast your people read. Measured adult silent reading of non-fiction averages about 238 words per minute, but measured **study** rates run from roughly 170 down to 40 wpm depending on the task (Brysbaert 2019, a meta-analysis of 190 studies). At ~450 words a page, the rows above work out to about 150, 225 and 260 wpm — so "~20 pages/hour" is a studying rate and "~35" is a reading rate. They're in the right places; just be honest about which one you're asking for.*

**Watch / listen** *(count at real length):*

| Activity | Rate |
| --- | --- |
| Recorded video / lecture | 1× its length (a 12-min video = 0.2 hr) + note time |
| Synchronous session / live demo | 1:1 (1 hr = 1 hr) |
| Podcast / audio | 1× its length |

**Hands-on (the part academic tools miss — estimate per rep, then tune):**

| Activity | Starting estimate |
| --- | --- |
| Guided hands-on rep (a brew, a build, one exercise) | ~15–20 min each |
| Practice / worksheet set | estimate by # of problems |
| Paired / group activity | real time it runs |

*Two of those rows can't be used at the §3.5 fit-check — "real time it runs" and a 1:1 live demo both need materials that don't exist until §6. That's expected: at §3.5 you're sizing, so estimate; at §6a step 6 you're budgeting, so measure.*

**Shared equipment changes the arithmetic.** If groups share a machine, a bench or a station, elapsed time isn't the length of the task — it's **machine-minutes × number of groups ÷ number of machines**. Three pairs sharing one mill at twenty minutes each is a sixty-minute block, not a twenty-minute one. Plan a bench task for the groups who are waiting, or that time becomes dead air.

*(The original academic rates — reflective response ~ 1 hr, peer responses ~ 2/hr — are kept here only for reference; most won't appear in a hands-on workshop.)*

## How long will this take *them*? Don't estimate — instrument.

The benchmarks above get you a starting number. They can't get you the right one, because the person estimating is the person who already knows how to do it.

**You will underestimate, and your expertise is the reason.** This is a measured effect, not a figure of speech: when experts, intermediates and novices were each asked to predict how long novices would take on a task, **the more expertise someone had, the worse their prediction was** — and standard debiasing instructions didn't fix it (Hinds, 1999, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*). People at an intermediate level were the most accurate. Worse, experts also write instructions at a higher level of abstraction, so the same expertise that mis-times the task also *specifies* it in a way that slows novices down (Hinds, Patterson & Pfeffer, 2001, *Journal of Applied Psychology*).

**Padding won't save you.** When thesis students predicted their own submission dates, the average prediction was 34 days and the average actual was 56. Fewer than half finished even by their own **worst-case** date — and predictions made deliberately pessimistically were no more *accurate* than the plain ones (Buehler, Griffin & Ross, 1994, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*). Adding 50% to a number you made up gives you a bigger made-up number.

**There is no multiplier to reach for.** You'd want a rule like "novices take three times as long." It doesn't exist. The measured expert-novice gap ranges from *no difference at all* to roughly fourfold depending on the domain and the task, and the famous "four times" figure comes from a study comparing **one** expert with **one** novice. Any general multiplier would be invention dressed as evidence.

**So do this instead:**

1. **Run the activity yourself with a clock running.** Write down the wall-clock time — don't trust the memory of it afterwards; recalled durations are themselves systematic underestimates (Roy, Christenfeld & McKenzie, 2005, *Psychological Bulletin*). That number is your **floor**, not your estimate.
2. **Put it in front of two or three people who actually resemble your learners**, and log their time from a timestamp. Don't ask them afterwards — in one study learners over-reported how long work took them 78% of the time, by a median factor of about 1.45, while 79% of them believed they tracked their time accurately (Hart, Warren & Edwards, 2025, *PLOS ONE*).
3. **Keep the ratio between your time and theirs.** That is your correction factor — for your domain, your materials, your people. It is the only one with any standing, because it's built from comparable cases rather than from a general rule.
4. **Write it down and reuse it.** By your third or fourth activity you're estimating from your own data instead of your intuition, which is the only correction this problem actually responds to.

*None of this is exotic: timing real learners is a standard step in systematic instructional design (the small-group stage of formative evaluation collects completion time) — it's just the step almost everyone skips. If you genuinely can't find two representative people, use the slowest plausible rate from the table above, state the result as a range rather than a number, call it provisional, and measure it the first time you run the course.*

## How to use it

1. **List every piece of work** in the module (readings, watch/listen, hands-on).
2. **Set an average-per-hour** for each from the benchmarks above (adjust for your people).
3. **Enter the total quantity.**
4. **Compute hours** (total ÷ average) and **sum.**
5. **Compare to the container** and adjust: overflow → split, cut depth (§7), or move to pre/post-class; too thin → merge or add practice.

## Worked example — Coffee "Setup & First Brew" (90-min workshop session)

| Type | Item | Avg/hr | Total | Hours |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Watch (pre) | Grind-size + freshness videos | 1× length | ~8 min | ~0.15 (pre-class) |
| Live | Welcome + one model + taste reference | 1:1 | 15 min | 0.25 |
| Live | Gear & beans walkthrough | 1:1 | 15 min | 0.25 |
| Hands-on | Brew-along demo | 1 brew/0.25 hr | 1 | 0.25 |
| Hands-on | Paired fill-in brew | 1 brew/0.3 hr | 1 | 0.30 |
| Hands-on | Two-cup compare + tasting | real time | 20 min | 0.35 |
| | | | **Live total** | **~1.4 hrs** |

**Read-out:** ~1.4 hrs of live activity against a 1.5-hr workshop block — fits, but tight once you add transitions. If it had hit 2 hrs, move the two-cup compare to post-class rather than cut it. Pre-class video (~0.15 hr) sits well under any reasonable pre-work cap.
